### Below is the learning engine for pattern recognition and execution
Study, don't solve
- Study the full worked solution and pattern for different problems
- This is the encoding step

- **What's the general move sequence of this pattern**
- **Why does this move sequence work? What condition allows this move sequence to work**

### Feeling Tired Before a Task
When you get tired before performing a task or learning session it's just your brain saying that the task is too much effort and it will choose to go the lazy route and that flagging of an effortful task is feeling tired. Its called cognitive aversion.

To combat this you need to reduce the barrier to entry. For example, attempting the problem and generating an answer and allowing yourself to be wrong and produce a rough answer instead of waiting to feel ready to produce a correct one. A rough first guess or attempt converts the task from generation to evaluation. Evaluation is a different cognitive operation with a much lower cost. Progress signals recruit dopamine circuitry which makes starting the hardest part of any task.

**Caused by**
- Mental resistance
	- Brain views upcoming task as a threat or too difficult so it makes you feel sleepy
- Decision fatigue
	- Small choices and planning the task before actually doing it burns your mental energy
- Stress response
	- Anticipating pressure or failure triggers a rush of stress and the nervous system responds by shutting down energy to cop

**Chemical Shift**
- Amygdala perceives stress - spike in cortisol and adenosine (fatigue)
- Striatum predicts no reward - drop in dopamine (no motivation)

### Tackling Problem Sets
#### Why you become stuck
- Retrieval Failure
	- You have the right concept/formula somewhere in memory, but you can't pull it up in this context. This happens when a problem is dressed up differently than how you learn the concept (Same problem, different variation).
- Missing schema
	- You genuinely haven't built a mental "problem type" category yet. Experts solve problems fast largely because they can instantly classify problems and that classification calls up a whole solution path. Beginners are stuck comparing the problem to nothing, so every problem feels novel even with its a minor variant of one they've seen
- Procedural gap
	- You know the setup and the concept, but a specific mechanical step (sign convention, algebra, unit conversion) breaks down partway through

"I don't know where to start" comes from a **missing schema**. Rereading the chapter or staring at the problem longer doesn't build a schema. Only exposure to solving patterns, compared across problems builds this.

**How do we get correct exposure to solving problems to build schemas?**
Comparison across multiple examples
- Asking yourself "What's the same about how I solved these, despite them looking different"
Interleaving
- Mixing in different problem types
Self explanation
- Pausing to explain why a step is valid, not just that it works
- You need to be able to explain why a step was taken to fully understand it 
- This you do through our **unstuck methods**

**Schema** - Schemas are mental blueprints you create to help you recognize underlying patterns in a problem/topic instead of just understanding the surface level information
- Schemas are used to identify the core structure of a problem immediately
- Gives you a preset of rules, formulas and steps to solve a specific problem type
- Strong schemas allow you to apply a solution method from a past problem to a brand new scenario with the same underlying structure
- Strong schemas allow you to fill in the gaps in hard question by predicting what missing information you need based on similar past problems


#### How to get unstuck and develop the schema
1. **Struggle first, genuinely, for a fixed time** (5–10 min is reasonable for a homework problem). Not endless flailing — but actively trying: draw the diagram, label knowns, guess the problem type. This struggle is not wasted even when it fails, for a specific reason explained below.
2. **If still stuck, look at only the _smallest possible unit_ of the solution** — ideally just enough to answer "what type of problem is this / what's the first move," then stop and cover the rest. Try to continue on your own from there.
3. **If you get stuck again, repeat: reveal one more small step, cover, retry.** This is called "graduated" or "faded" worked-example use in the learning literature — you're taking exactly as much scaffolding as needed and no more.
4. **Only if you're stuck at nearly every step do you read the full solution.** In that case, read it once for understanding (not memorizing), then close it completely.
5. **Immediately after closing it, redo the entire problem from a blank page, no peeking.** This is the step people skip, and it's the single highest-value step in the whole process.
6. **If you can't complete it cleanly on that redo, look again, then redo again.** Repeat until you can do it start-to-finish unaided.
7. **The real test of "learned it" isn't the redo right after — it's redoing the same problem (or a close variant) 1–2 days later, cold.** If you can still do it, it's moved into more durable memory. If you can't, it tells you the first success was shallow (you were pattern-matching your own recent solution, not truly reconstructing understanding).

#### Maintaining What You Learned
- Stick to the recall schedule for concepts and problem sets
- Once problem sets stick they can match the same schedule so do recall and problem sets on the same days that you were supposed to do recalls
- If you're tight on time pick the problems you had the hardest time with 
- Because you're doing the same problems again we need to make sure we're actually understanding and not just memorizing. This is where it's important to go into detail and explain why we took a certain step and what the reason was for it
- If you're not able to retrieve the information then make sure you reduce the spacing of recall until you get it right
